## Blog Post Week 12

There's a tension at the heart of open source collaboration that I don't see discussed enough. There is a weird line between shared infrastructure and soft antitrust.
<!--more-->

Take something like Automotive Grade Linux. On paper, it's a win for everyone: car companies pool resources to build a shared software platform instead of each reinventing the wheel. Less duplicated effort, faster innovation, all the usual open source talking points. And that's genuinely true — there's real societal efficiency in not having five companies independently solving the same solved problem.

But zoom out a little and it starts to look different. When the three biggest automakers co-develop an open source platform that assumes specific hardware, what exactly is the difference between that and cost-sharing that raises barriers to entry? The platform is "open," but if the ecosystem is built around components new entrants can't easily access, openness becomes a technicality. You get the PR benefits of open source and the competitive moat of a closed system at the same time. It's a neat trick, and I'm not sure the current antitrust framework is equipped to think about it clearly.


On a completely different axis, open source is also just *really hard* at the individual level. Last week only 1-2 PRs were merged into main based on class discussion, and it seems we're all stuck on trying to get this one maintainer's attention for our projects. I perhaps got lucky because late this week my PR was merged into main for some bug fix/compatibility issues, but larger feature fixes seem harder to do (but what we want to do).

What's been interesting to watch lately is the rise of AI-generated pull requests or Clawdbot-style contributions that are clearly automated. Tthe same open source projects that can barely handle human contributors are now fielding a wave of machine-generated ones, and the incentives on both sides are strange enough that it's actually working in some cases.
